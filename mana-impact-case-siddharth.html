Part 1: Currency Architecture for a Multi-Currency Impact Fund

Data Modelling · Systems Design · Management Reporting

Understanding the Case

This case centres on designing a financial data model for an organisation that receives donations, moves funds internally across programmes, and disburses to NGOs. While it conducts transactions in multiple currencies, USD and SGD specifically, it reports in SGD. The proposed data model must address three complexities:

Fund Visibility. Who are our funders, how much have they contributed, which programmes or focus areas are funded, and what is our current balance across funds? A good model will make these questions answerable at a glance, through a live, structured view of the data.

Reconciliation. Without a clear transaction model to distinguish inflows, internal transfers, and outflows, double-counting becomes likely and gaps become invisible. A well-designed system shows a running balance at all times, and makes it obvious when something does not reconcile.

Multi-Currency Reporting. The organisation transacts in USD and SGD but reports primarily in SGD. Every transaction needs a reliable SGD equivalent that is consistent enough for reporting while being transparent about variance when actual settlement rates differ from the estimate. The architecture needs to handle this gracefully without requiring a finance team to manually reconcile every line item.

The Data Model

Transactions. Every movement of money is a single row in the Transactions table. The Type field (Inflow, Internal Transfer, or Outflow) is the key design decision: it gives you a complete fund flow history without double-counting. The two SGD columns handle foreign exchange, one estimated and one actual, giving you both reporting consistency and real-world accuracy.

Entity tables. Three supporting tables represent the parties involved in each transaction. Funders are linked to every Inflow. Recipients are linked to every Outflow. Funds/Programmes are linked to Internal Transfers, tracking the internal buckets money moves through. Together they answer the Fund Visibility question: who gave, who received, and where did money sit in between.

Exchange Rates. A simple reference table with one entry per month per currency pair. The Transactions table pulls from this automatically to populate the Estimated SGD field. If the organisation has a fixed or negotiated rate with their bank, this table holds a single static rate instead. A 5% variance threshold between estimated and actual SGD flags anything worth investigating.

Assumptions

  • This model is designed for management reporting, not accounting. The estimated SGD fields allow us to see flows at a glance even when actual exchange data isn't updated.
  • For precise accounting, actual SGD fields need to be updated per transaction. If the firm transacts at a fixed bank rate, this can be applied uniformly across all records.
  • Three transaction types are assumed: Inflows, Internal Transfers, and Outflows. Investment income or interest on held cash would be added as a fourth type if needed.
  • The model scales beyond USD and SGD. Adding a new currency requires only a new row in the Exchange Rates table.

Suggested Tech Stack: Database: Airtable  |  Automation: n8n or Make.com

Part 2: Newsletter and Event Management

Systems Design · CRM Integration · Marketing Automation

Understanding the Case

This case centres on a common but easily mishandled integration: a contact database and an email marketing platform that need to stay in sync without spawning multiple sources of truth.

My core design principle: the database owns the data, Mailchimp owns the sending. To maintain the boundary cleanly, we use deliberate architecture at every touchpoint: new sign-ups, preference updates, segmentation, and unsubscribes.

The Architecture

Data flow. Data flows in one direction: from the database to Mailchimp. Every contact record originates in the database, is enriched there, and is pushed to Mailchimp as a read-only audience.

Deduplication. Deduplication happens at the database level using email address as the unique identifier. Two forms handle this: a sign-up form for new contacts, and an "update your preferences" form linked below every email. Both use upsert logic: if the incoming email matches an existing record, it updates rather than duplicates. Fields are only overwritten if the incoming value is non-empty, so a contact updating their geography will not accidentally clear their donor tier or interest area.

Segmentation. All segmentation fields (donor tier, geography, interest area, consent flags) are pushed to Mailchimp as merge fields. Segmentation is handled natively in Mailchimp, no segments need to be pre-built in the database.

Consent. Consent is tracked as two fields in the database: newsletter consent and event invitation consent. A contact can hold both, either, or neither. Mailchimp never writes back to the database, with one essential exception: if a contact unsubscribes directly via Mailchimp, a webhook updates their consent fields in the database immediately. Without this, the next sync would re-add them and override their unsubscribe.

Assumptions

  • Email address is the unique identifier for all contacts.
  • Segmentation data (geography, interest areas) is already present for contacts in the database.
